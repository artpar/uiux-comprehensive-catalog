# UX learning curriculum: editorial blueprint

This is the teaching plan for a professional UX learning path. It is broader than the existing pattern catalog: learners need explanations, examples, methods, guided practice, feedback, and opportunities to apply ideas to new interfaces. The six cases in `UX_PLATFORM_SAMPLE_CASES.md` are practice anchors, not substitutes for instruction.

## Learning promise

Develop the ability to understand an experience in context, recognize and investigate problems, create and test improvements, and communicate defensible decisions across digital, voice, physical, and service interactions. Finishing the path demonstrates practice with these abilities; it does not confer professional certification or imply universal mastery.

## Teaching pattern for every lesson

1. **Purpose:** Explain why this capability matters in professional work and what the learner will be able to do.
2. **Teach:** Present the concept in plain language, including its limits and common misconceptions. This is substantive reading or media, not a prompt to guess.
3. **Show:** Walk through an expert's reasoning on one example, including evidence, alternatives, and tradeoffs.
4. **Guide:** Let the learner practice with hints and feedback tied to the reasoning, not just a correct/incorrect label.
5. **Apply:** Give a new context with less guidance. Let the learner produce an artifact when the skill calls for one and assess it with a rubric that accepts defensible alternatives.
6. **Revisit:** Bring the principle back later in another modality so the learner practices transfer and can correct misunderstandings.

This progression follows the instructional model of demonstration, coached practice, and increasing independence described by [Carnegie Mellon's Eberly Center](https://www.cmu.edu/teaching/designteach/teach/instructionalstrategies/labsstudios.html). The subject progression is informed by the [Design Council's Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver framework](https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/resources/the-double-diamond/), but the course is organized around abilities rather than a rigid project process.

## Part 1 — Understand experience quality

**Outcome:** Explain quality in terms of users, tasks, context, accessibility, clarity, trust, and consequences, rather than appearance alone.

| Lesson | Teach explicitly | Learner produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. What counts as an experience? | UX across interfaces, services, time, and handoffs; user goals versus organizational goals. | A map of one user's complete task, including touchpoints outside a screen. |
| 2. What makes an interaction understandable? | Feedback, signifiers, mapping, constraints, system status, and recovery; when these principles conflict. | An explanation of how a user knows what happened and what to do next. |
| 3. Who can complete the task? | Accessibility and inclusion across vision, hearing, motor, language, attention, device, and environment; avoid assuming one modality works for everyone. | A list of potential barriers and accessible alternatives for a scenario. |
| 4. What makes it trustworthy? | Honest status, reversible action, privacy, evidence, and the consequences of error. | A quality critique that distinguishes strengths, risks, and unknowns. |

**Guided practice anchor:** Appointment check-in kiosk. First show a worked critique of a different touchpoint, then ask learners to inspect the kiosk.

## Part 2 — Diagnose and frame problems

**Outcome:** Move from an observed symptom to a defensible problem statement without inventing causes.

| Lesson | Teach explicitly | Learner produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Observe the task | User goals, environment, constraints, workarounds, and moments of uncertainty. | Observation notes separated from interpretation. |
| 2. Gather evidence | Interviews, task observation, support reports, analytics, and accessibility checks; what each can and cannot establish. | A small research plan matched to a decision. |
| 3. Find the cause | Competing hypotheses, state distinctions, triangulation, and how to avoid solution-first framing. | A cause map with known facts and open questions. |
| 4. Define the problem | Affected users, task, failure, consequence, evidence, and priority. | A concise problem statement and an explicit uncertainty. |

**Guided practice anchor:** “No trips found” in a mobile planner. Teach empty data, no matching results, and system failure before asking learners to diagnose the message.

## Part 3 — Design and apply

**Outcome:** Improve a journey through deliberate alternatives and complete interaction states.

| Lesson | Teach explicitly | Learner produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Shape the journey | Entry, decision, action, feedback, recovery, and handoff across channels. | A task flow with both successful and interrupted paths. |
| 2. Generate alternatives | Use patterns as options; compare task fit, risk, accessibility, and implementation constraints. | Two plausible approaches and why one is better in context. |
| 3. Design every state | Required, pending, empty, error, permission, stale, success, and reversal states. | A state model and key interface text. |
| 4. Prototype the decision | Fidelity matched to the question; interaction behavior, content, and accessibility checks. | A small prototype or annotated flow with assumptions to test. |

**Guided practice anchor:** Enterprise approval request. Teach state and role modeling with a worked example before the learner redesigns the request.

## Part 4 — Test and improve

**Outcome:** Choose an evaluation method, learn from evidence, and revise an interaction responsibly.

| Lesson | Teach explicitly | Learner produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. State a hypothesis | What change should help whom, in which task, and how it might fail. | A falsifiable design hypothesis. |
| 2. Choose a method | Usability task, interview, accessibility review, field observation, experiment, and analytics; method limits. | A brief test plan with representative tasks. |
| 3. Interpret findings | Observation versus inference; severity, frequency, sample limitations, and conflicting evidence. | A findings summary with confidence and open questions. |
| 4. Iterate | Decide what to change, what to preserve, and what to retest. | A revised flow linked to specific findings. |

**Guided practice anchor:** Voice control of home heating. Teach multimodal test design before asking learners to revise the ambiguous response.

## Part 5 — Practice professionally

**Outcome:** Make and communicate UX decisions that survive real constraints, multiple roles, and service channels.

| Lesson | Teach explicitly | Learner produces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Work with constraints | Time, technology, operations, safety, regulation, business goals, and uncertainty. | A staged recommendation with explicit tradeoffs. |
| 2. Work across channels | Consistent information, ownership, transitions, fallback paths, and staff or service dependencies. | A cross-channel journey and responsibility map. |
| 3. Communicate decisions | Evidence, alternatives, recommendation, risks, and next test; tailoring detail to the audience. | A short decision memo and presentation outline. |
| 4. Capstone | Combine investigation, design, evaluation, and communication in an unfamiliar context. | A reviewable case study with evidence, revisions, and unresolved questions. |

**Capstone anchor:** Transit disruption across app, kiosk, audio, and staff. Treat the supplied scenario as an editorial prototype; add stronger domain evidence and expert review before publication.

**Cross-cutting AI case:** The support-reply copilot in `UX_PLATFORM_SAMPLE_CASES.md` applies Parts 1–5 to an AI-assisted web workflow. Teach source quality, uncertainty, human authority, review, and correction before its practice task. Return to the case after the learner has studied evaluation and professional communication so it functions as a transfer exercise, not as the only treatment of AI-assisted UX.

## Assessment and reference rules

- Early lessons can use recognition questions, but later work must require explanation, artifact creation, and revision.
- Guided decisions need feedback for plausible weaker choices, not only an ideal answer. Feedback should show why an answer is strong or weak, identify what evidence could change the answer, and link to the relevant source-backed reference.
- Guided options should represent credible tradeoffs, vary the strongest answer's position, and ask for the learner's reason and confidence. A lucky selection is not evidence of understanding.
- Each lesson needs an independent application in a different context and a reviewable artifact. Use the evidence, alternatives, consequences, accessibility, and uncertainty rubric in the sample cases as a starting point; refine it for each skill.
- Test-and-refine lessons must provide findings for the learner to interpret and require a revised interaction linked to those findings, followed by a proposed retest.
- Progress records completed instruction and practice separately. A person can read material without claiming demonstrated skill.
- The existing catalog is a reference library inside the learning journey. The course must teach research, synthesis, and professional communication that pattern entries alone do not cover.
- Vary contexts and modalities. Do not suggest that one interface convention transfers unchanged to voice, physical controls, or service interactions.
